# Haute & Hazard — Design Origin & Inspiration Guide

**Audience:** playtesters, publishers, reviewers, and collaborators.

This document explains **how Haute & Hazard came together, what each major system is trying to evoke, and which outside works or traditions helped inspire the design language**. It is meant to make playtest feedback more useful and publisher conversations more transparent.

> **Important:** Inspiration does not mean replication. Haute & Hazard combines influences into its own rules, characters, scoring, deck-building economy, Stage structure, battle system, and drag-centered game world. Anything that may require specific legal/IP review is marked **†** and tracked in [`ATTRIBUTIONS.md`](ATTRIBUTIONS.md) and [`IP_REVIEW_REGISTER.md`](IP_REVIEW_REGISTER.md).

## How the idea actually started

The project began in a very specific sequence.

Jake was **watching the *Aikatsu!* anime** and became interested in the way fashion, Brands, outfits, and performance were treated as part of the characters' identities. That led him to discover the **Data Carddass Aikatsu!** arcade/card system behind the franchise, where fashion cards are selected and coordinated for performances.

Later, after getting into the **DC Deck-Building Game**, the missing tabletop structure clicked into place: the fashion-card idea could be combined with a deck-building progression where players start small, acquire better cards during play, and gradually create a more distinctive personal deck.

From there, the concept changed substantially. The question became:

> **What if a fashion-coordinate card game and a deck-builder were rebuilt around drag performance, nightlife, competition, shopping, and direct rivalry?**

That became the core identity of **drag + fashion coordination + deck-building + performance + rivalry**.

The design goal that followed was:

> **Make drag performance matter mechanically, not just aesthetically.**

Instead of applying drag as a surface theme to an unrelated card game, the design asks what players do in a competitive drag-performance fantasy: build a look, shop for stronger pieces, develop a persona, perform for a room, earn audience response, confront rivals, survive mishaps, and leave the night with the strongest reputation.

## Aikatsu! anime → Data Carddass Aikatsu! — the fashion-game spark

One of the clearest structural inspirations is **Bandai's Data Carddass Aikatsu! arcade collectible card game**. In that game, players use cards representing clothes and accessories to dress a character for auditions, and the quality of the outfit contributes to performance. Early card lists visibly organize cards by fashion category, style/type, Brand, and Appeal Points.

Haute & Hazard takes inspiration from that high-level idea—**fashion pieces as gameplay objects with categories, style identity, Brands, and performance value**—and transforms it into a competitive tabletop deck-builder centered on drag.

Specific Aikatsu!-related concepts that helped shape the design direction include:

- fashion broken into equipable categories;
- identifiable fashion Brands;
- performance value attached directly to fashion cards;
- coordinating a full outfit as a meaningful gameplay goal;
- fashion being central to play rather than decorative flavor.

Haute & Hazard deliberately changes the implementation:

- core slots are **Face, Wig, Body, and Shoes**, with Accessory optional;
- players build a persistent tabletop **Coordinate** instead of scanning arcade cards;
- original Tenets and original Brands define the game's fashion identities;
- Appeal interacts with Matching, Perfect Illusion, Fusion, Stages, Queen abilities, and Shopping;
- cards enter through a deck-building economy rather than arcade collection/scanning;
- Gross SP, Stage trophies, penalties, asymmetric Queens, direct rivalry, and Dragdagulan† are part of Haute & Hazard's own tabletop structure.

### Terminology review note

Because **Appeal** and **Special Appeal†** are also terms visibly associated with Aikatsu!, their continued use in Haute & Hazard should be reviewed before commercial publication even though the implementation is materially different. The current prototype may continue using them for testing; the project does not claim those terms originated here.

## DC Deck-Building Game — the deck-building bridge

After the Aikatsu!/Carddass idea established the fashion side, the **DC Deck-Building Game** helped provide the tabletop lens for progression: begin with a basic deck, acquire stronger/more specialized cards during play, and let the player's deck become more individual over the course of the session.

Haute & Hazard does not use DC characters, card names, art, or its specific licensed setting. The influence is the broader deck-building structure and the experience of a deck changing during play.

## Why deck-building?

Deck-building tells a story of **transformation during one game**.

Players begin with the same modest 12-card Dressing Room Floor deck. As they earn Tips and shop, their decks become increasingly personal. A Queen's identity emerges from both her asymmetric Queen card and the wardrobe the player builds during play.

Design intent:

- early game = working with limited resources;
- midgame = discovering a fashion/Brand direction;
- late game = a deck that increasingly reflects the player's choices;
- every purchase should feel like adding something to the Queen's repertoire.

## Why a persistent Coordinate?

Many deck-builders send purchased cards back into the deck and leave little visible evidence of what the player has built. Haute & Hazard adds the **Coordinate** so fashion has a physical presence on the table.

Face, Wig, Body, and Shoes form the core silhouette; Accessory can embellish it. The Coordinate persists between turns, so the player is not merely improving probability—they are **constructing a Look**.

This system combines fashion-coordinate inspiration with tableau-building: the player's choices remain visible and continue to matter.

## Why Tenets and Brands?

Tenets and Brands give fashion coordination both a **broad visual identity** and a **more specific strategic identity**.

- **Tenets** are the broad color/faction language.
- **Brands** are named style lines inside those Tenets.

This gives players two layers of recognition: a Look can broadly belong together while still caring about a particular aesthetic line.

## Why Tips and Shopping?

Tips turn audience response into purchasing power. The shared **Wardrobe Rack** and finite **Thrift Store** make shopping visible and competitive.

Thematic loop:

> **applause becomes money → money becomes wardrobe → wardrobe becomes performance.**

Mechanical goals:

- create timing pressure around purchases;
- make opponents care about what appears in the market;
- make deck-building feel like shopping rather than abstract card acquisition.

## Why Appeal†?

Appeal† is the Queen's current performance strength on the active Stage. It represents the effect of the full Look and active performance rather than permanent victory points.

The game separates four resources on purpose:

- **Tips** = buying power;
- **Appeal†** = this performance;
- **Gross SP** = score/reputation accumulated across the night;
- **LS** = battle strength during a Lip Sync confrontation.

A Queen can therefore be rich in Tips, strong on the Stage, ahead in score, or dangerous in battle without those being the same thing.

## Why Matching, Perfect Illusion, and Fusion?

These Look states represent three different fashion fantasies.

- **Matching** rewards commitment to a coherent Tenet.
- **Perfect Illusion** rewards a Look aligned with the Queen's own Signature Tenet.
- **Fusion** rewards intentionally combining exactly two non-Neutral Tenets.

The goal is to avoid one universally correct way to dress. Players can pursue purity, character alignment, or deliberate contrast.

## Why asymmetric Queens?

Drag personas are highly individual, so the game would lose much of its identity if every player differed only by their deck.

Each Queen therefore has:

- a Signature Tenet;
- a Favorite Brand;
- a Signature Ability;
- one **Special Appeal†** usable once per game.

The Queen card gives the player a starting point, but deck-building still allows a pivot away from that identity.

## Why venue-style Stages?

The Stage deck evolved toward **places rather than generic challenge names** because venues make the game world feel like a night moving through different performance spaces.

The current roster includes a ballroom, nightclub, haunted hotel, conservatory, thrift superstore, cathedral, opera house, leather bar, candy pavilion, boardwalk, couture runway, and blacklight arcade.

Mechanically, each venue pushes players toward different Tenets, Brands, objectives, and bonuses. The goal is for the active Stage to change decisions rather than merely set a target number.

## Why Judges, Spotlight, and Brand Ovation?

The advanced Stage layer models the idea that a performance is judged on **more than raw Appeal†**.

- Judge / Spotlight Requirement creates a situational objective.
- Judge's Favor rewards satisfying that objective.
- Brand Ovation rewards arriving in exactly the right fashion language for the venue.

Because that information can overwhelm a first game, Beginner Mode temporarily removes this layer while preserving the same cards and core systems.

## Why Beginner Mode?

Beginner Mode follows a teaching principle:

> **simplify the information layer, not the identity of the game.**

New players still learn Shopping, Coordinates, Tenets/Brands, Matching, Perfect Illusion, Fusion, Queens, Slay, and Dragdagulan†. Only the advanced Judge/Spotlight scoring layer is ignored.

## Why Dragdagulan†?

The game needed a moment where rivalry becomes direct rather than indirect.

**Dragdagulan†** is the current working name for the head-to-head Lip Sync battle system. The term is credited to *Drag Den Philippines* and is subject to legal/IP review before commercial release.

The battle implementation was designed for Haute & Hazard:

- challenge an eligible rival;
- each player draws three battle cards from their personal deck;
- total printed LS plus explicit battle modifiers;
- winner earns a meaningful reward;
- loser risks a penalty.

Equipped Fashion does not automatically provide battle LS. This keeps the battle tied to **what the player built into their deck**, not simply who currently has the strongest Coordinate.

## Why penalties?

Wardrobe Malfunction, The Chop, and Extermination create the possibility that a glamorous night leaves scars.

They provide:

- consequences for rivalry and card effects;
- end-game pressure because penalties reduce Final Score;
- thematic reminders that the night can go wrong.

## Why Curtain Call and Final Judging?

The game is framed as a night of performances rather than a race to a single objective.

**Curtain Call** closes a Stage and resets the table for the next performance. **Final Judging** asks players to look back across the whole night: Gross SP, bonuses, penalties, trophies, and tiebreakers.

The result is a beginning-middle-end structure:

**Dressing Room → repeated venue performances → Final Judging.**

## Why the experimental two-placement Stage rule?

The alternate Stage Winner / Runner-Up module exists because playtesting raised a design question: could Dragdagulan† become more naturally connected to Stage competition?

The experimental flow is:

**Stage Winner → finish the round → Runner-Up → optional Runner-Up Dragdagulan† → Curtain Call.**

It remains non-canonical until repeated testing shows whether it improves drama and engagement without slowing the game or weakening first place.

## Why physical + TTS parity?

The Game Crafter prototype and Tabletop Simulator build test the **same game**, not two parallel versions.

Physical play exposes handling, readability, table-presence, and production issues. TTS supports remote testing and publisher demonstrations. Keeping them aligned lets feedback be compared cleanly.

## What the current physical prototype represents

The current Game Crafter build uses:

- **Rules** — Medium Folio Set;
- **Haute & Hazard** — 240-card Poker Deck;
- **Stages** — 12-card Foil Euro Poker Deck;
- **The Queens** — 12-card Jumbo Deck;
- **Player Aid** — five Postcard Mats;
- **Box** — Medium Prototype Box.

These are prototype-production choices, not promises about final retail manufacturing. A publisher may change sizes, counts, materials, or packaging.

## What remains open

Haute & Hazard is not presented as finished. Current playtesting is specifically allowed to challenge:

- pacing;
- Queen balance;
- Stage complexity;
- Dragdagulan† frequency and payoff;
- terminology;
- component count and cost;
- visual hierarchy;
- rules wording;
- art direction;
- the experimental two-placement Stage structure.

The core goal is more stable than any one implementation:

> **Make players feel that building a drag Look, shopping for fashion, performing for a venue, and confronting rivals are all parts of one connected game.**

## For playtesters

When something feels strange, do not only ask whether it is balanced. Ask whether the mechanic delivers the fantasy described above.

Useful questions:

- Does Shopping feel like hunting for the next piece of a Look?
- Does a Complete Coordinate feel satisfying when it comes together?
- Does the Stage change what you want to wear?
- Does a Queen feel like a distinct persona?
- Does Dragdagulan† feel like an escalation of rivalry?
- Does Final Judging feel like the end of a night rather than arithmetic?

That feedback is often more useful than a proposed numerical fix.

## For publishers

The project is designed to be **developable**. The theme-mechanic relationship is the part the designer most wants to preserve; terminology, component engineering, balance, art direction, production form, and individual subsystems remain open to editorial and publisher development.
